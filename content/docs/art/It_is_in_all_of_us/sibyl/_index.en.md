# Sibyl

## Known historical context

In ancient Greece, the sibyls were prophetesses. They spoke in the name of God, Apollo, entered trance and could predict
the future. Their messages were written in hexameters. The first known sibyl sang from a rock at Delphi eleven centuries
before Christ. Several sibyls have been identified: the one from Cumas, the one from Eritrea, the one from Delphi, the
one from Tibur, etc. They prophesied in Apollo’s temples. Mythologically, they were daughters of Zeus and, on occasion,
naiads, that is, nymphs of freshwater bodies. The sibyls lived in caves near rivers.

When Constantine converted the Empire to Christianity, he used the figure of the sibyls as a bridge, arguing that they
had announced the coming of Jesus, the day of the final judgment and the end of the world. This was a political strategy
to facilitate the conversion of pagans.

In the following centuries, the sibyls’ texts were translated into Latin and interpreted by various saints, and several
musical versions were created. The earliest evidence of the interpretation of the Sibyl’s Song in Mallorca dates back to
1363.

The figure of the Sibyl is so important that Michelangelo painted her in the Sistine Chapel (1508-1512).

A few decades later (1563), the Council of Trent prohibited the Sibyl’s Song as part of the liturgy. In Mallorca, the
resolution was only observed for two years.

## Interpretation

How could one worship women who spoke in the name of God, could read and write, were consulted to resolve state matters,
and whose prophecies were so important that they were preserved in writing for centuries?

It is not a worship of a goddess, but of flesh‑and‑bone women who were recognized for their ability to channel. With
great political influence. An etymology suggests that *sibyl* means “divine counsel,” although it is not fully accepted.

These women did not appear in Greece eleven centuries before Christ nor disappeared when Constantine stripped all power
from Delphi and moved “the center of the world” to Constantinople. These women have always existed and remain here.
Sometimes they are called witches, other prophets, other artists.

What was happening in Mallorca before the genocide of the conquest that gave a female figure so much power that even the
Council of Trent could not stop its worship or veneration?

In my research on art and magic, the sibyls have whispered a path that traverses between the natural and the ancestral,
through water and trees. A path that is drawn on maps and, starting from Mallorca, where her song still resonates, has
led me close to Tivoli and its forest. It seems to point toward Crete and Turkey. Crete, a possible origin of the cult
of Dionysus, and Turkey, Anatolia, for its relation to the Eritrean Sibyl, whose prophecies are the source of the
Sibyl’s Song.

The Sibyl, as a mythical figure, is a convergence point between various symbolic and spiritual elements that intertwine
ancestral wisdom and connection with universal energetic flows.

In my interpretation, the Sibyl acts as a catalyst similar to the heart chakra, reflecting the place where energies
converge to transform, heal, and unify. She is not just a static symbol, but a living figure of transformation:
intuition, ancestral knowledge, and connection with cosmic and earthly energy are integrated in her and in our hearts to
transform reality.

Her techniques and language resonate with the cult of Dionysus, Tantra, and Cherokee traditions.

The sibyls, as possibly hereditary prophetesses, worked in temples and entered trance and ecstasy to contact Apollo and
the underworld, connecting them with Dionysian and tantric priestesses.

My [research](..) arises from the need to connect deeply and symbolically with the wisdom that traverses territories,
time, and cultures.

## Sibyls and Oracles in Art

Delphi is an oracular epicenter. History tells us that the Pythia were the Apollo‑oriented priestesses who directed the
temple. They entered trance with the help of vapors and rituals to make their predictions. Their channelings were
written and interpreted by priests in a clearly defined ritual. They were chosen among the women of Delphi and had to
meet a series of moral requirements.

The sibyls were women of the people who channeled divine messages in a trance state. They were often considered
wanderers. Their prophecies tended to announce disasters. Their trace goes back to a time before Delphi and they could
have come from Persia or the Far East.

Some sources say it is a historical error to confuse them. Whether or not it is an error, both the Pythia and the sibyls
were prophetesses who entered trance to speak in the name of God. The Pythia had a high social rank and direct
participation in political life, but even the Sibyls were respected. In the framework of my research, sibyls and Pythia
are the same: women channelers who link the visible and invisible world, day and night or sun and moon. The connection
between sky and earth.

Expanding even further the idea, the Dionysian priestesses, the Minoan priestesses, and the priestesses of Hathor are
the same women. A voice that has accompanied us since the beginning of humanity and has wanted to be silenced through
persecution, murders, and discrediting.

However, their power has always slipped through the cracks and art has portrayed them for centuries. In fact, in the
case of some mediums, art has been their form of expression.
